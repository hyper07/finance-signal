## 1. International Journal of Forecasting — special issue "The Future of Forecasting: Bridging Theory and Practice"

Dear Guest Editors,

I am submitting *When the News Arrives: Calibration Failure of a Pattern‑Based Seven‑Session Forecast Around Exogenous Information Shocks* for the special issue. The paper audits a **deployed** forecasting product — a seven‑session historical‑analog forecaster publishing immutable daily signals since January 2026 — by regenerating every point‑in‑time forecast it could have issued with the production code, across a cryptocurrency pair, the S&P 500 pair and 50 single stocks (≈1.5 million scored origin‑by‑horizon forecasts, 204,129 of them next‑session stock forecasts).

The paper speaks to three of the issue's themes. *Trustworthy forecasting:* it shows that interval calibration and directional skill can diverge (calibrated bands, no skill), that directional accuracy is base‑rate inflated on drifting assets, and that a 50‑origin evaluator prints ≥60% on some horizon 14% of days by noise alone. *Open science and benchmarking:* all rules were pre‑specified, every p‑value is FDR‑controlled, and the protocol re‑runs in ten minutes from a deposited archive. *Bridging theory and practice:* elementary propositions bound the accuracy of any price‑conditioned predictor by the frequency of information arrivals — here 3–5% of sessions carry 28–37% of return variance — and a causal, event‑conditional gate is tested out of sample as the constructive counterpart.

The manuscript is not under consideration elsewhere. A preprint is on arXiv ([arXiv ID]). I operate the service whose forecasts are audited; this is disclosed in the paper and the code and data manifest are released for third‑party verification.

Sincerely, Kibaek Kim

## 2. Quantitative Finance (alternative first choice for the full paper)

Dear Editors,

Please consider *When the News Arrives* for publication in *Quantitative Finance*. The paper evaluates a live seven‑session forecaster on 1,125–4,097 point‑in‑time origins per instrument across three asset types, conditions on exogenous events defined three independent ways (return shocks, headline spikes, public calendars), and proves elementary bounds that explain the sign of the failure it finds: a nearest‑neighbour forecaster is contrarian into news‑driven moves that continue. It quantifies the resulting predictability gap, shows that the same engine fails differently on crypto, an index and single stocks, and tests a type‑specific gate out of sample.

The paper is empirical and negative for the forecaster; its contribution is the audit protocol, the exogenous conditioning, and the type‑specific evidence. It is not under consideration elsewhere; a preprint is on arXiv ([arXiv ID]). Conflict of interest is disclosed in the manuscript.

Sincerely, Kibaek Kim

## 3. Journal of Financial Data Science (alternative for the full paper)

Dear Editors,

*When the News Arrives* audits a deployed ML forecasting product on its own frozen live signals — a setting your readers build and operate but rarely see evaluated end to end. Using the production code, exhaustive point‑in‑time scoring and pre‑specified event definitions, it shows where and why the product's seven‑session forecasts fail (information arrivals it cannot see; a contrarian analog pool; base‑rate‑inflated accuracy displays), quantifies the ceiling any price‑conditioned model faces, compares how users should act on a multi‑day schedule (daily, weekly, once — none dominates), and tests a causal, event‑conditional gate as the fix. The pipeline re‑runs in ten minutes from a deposited archive.

Not under consideration elsewhere; preprint on arXiv ([arXiv ID]); author operates the audited service (disclosed).

## 4. Finance Research Letters

Dear Editors,

I submit *Confidently wrong on the news: a live Bitcoin forecaster around information shocks* as a short article. Using the production code of a deployed forecaster and its immutable live signals, it shows that the model is interval‑calibrated but directionally uninformative in calm conditions and systematically on the wrong side of information shocks — 37% next‑session accuracy the day before a |z|≥2.5 move, 39% on headline‑spike days, zero coverage — while prices drift a further 3% over the week and the model's consensus needs six sessions to agree. Five percent of sessions carry 35% of return variance, which bounds any price‑conditioned forecaster near the coin. The 2026‑08‑19 White House crypto summit is analysed as the case. The letter is 2,500 words with two tables and one figure; the full multi‑asset study is a separate working paper (arXiv [arXiv ID]) and the two do not overlap in reported results beyond the method summary.

Sincerely, Kibaek Kim

## 5. Journal of Behavioral and Experimental Finance

Dear Editors,

*After the News* studies how prices and people move after information shocks and how a deployed forecasting model responds. Across a cryptocurrency pair, an equity index and 50 stocks it documents type‑specific post‑shock behaviour — continuation in crypto (+3.0% over seven sessions), post‑earnings drift in stocks (+2.3%), reversal on market‑wide index days — the model's latency in agreeing with the move (4–6 sessions), and a simulation of how a user should act on a seven‑session exposure schedule (daily, weekly or once), where no cadence dominates. A catalogue of 346 single‑stock increases with news attribution shows every one to be an information event. The paper closes with a research agenda on news, social media and the neuroscience of collective reaction. It complements a separate forecast‑evaluation paper (arXiv [arXiv ID]) and reports distinct results.

Sincerely, Kibaek Kim

## 6. Frontiers in Behavioral Neuroscience (Perspective)

Dear Editors,

I submit *Why price‑pattern forecasters fail when people react* as a Perspective. It argues, from an audit of a deployed financial forecaster across 54 instruments, that the failure of machine‑learning price forecasters on news days is the absence from their state space of a human process — attention, arousal and social influence — and proposes a neuroeconomic measurement program (reaction‑time and arousal studies with social cues, separation of forced from discretionary flow, longitudinal panels) to estimate the population response kernel that such models need. The empirical results are published separately (arXiv [arXiv ID]); this piece is the agenda.

Sincerely, Kibaek Kim
